## Purpose

Create work that feels like it comes from a small, highly technical institutional
investment firm rather than a conventional crypto startup, generic fintech company,
luxury wealth manager, or large corporate asset manager.

The desired impression is:

**quiet competence, cold precision, alpine permanence, engineered restraint.**

The work should feel sophisticated because of what has been removed, not because
more visual effects have been added.

---

# Core Design Idea

Combine two visual forces:

**Natural monumentality**
- mountains
- exposed rock
- snow
- atmospheric distance
- geological scale
- physical permanence

with:

**Engineered precision**
- hard geometry
- grids
- exact alignment
- restrained typography
- controlled spacing
- structured information

The tension between these two elements is the core of the aesthetic.

Do not make everything geometric.
Do not make everything photographic.

The contrast is the brand.

---

# Taste Test

A successful design should feel closer to:

- a specialist systematic fund
- an institutional research desk
- Swiss modernist editorial design
- an alpine expedition archive
- a highly technical investment boutique

than to:

- a DeFi protocol
- a Web3 startup
- a private bank
- a consulting firm
- a luxury brand
- a generic SaaS company
- a motivational finance brand

The implied organization should feel like:

> A small group of unusually technical people managing something difficult.

---

# Primary Visual Principles

## 1. Restraint before decoration

When choosing between adding and removing an element, default to removing it.

Every visible object needs a job.

Avoid:
- decorative lines without information value
- unnecessary icons
- gradients for atmosphere
- floating cards for their own sake
- excessive badges
- ornamental patterns
- unnecessary borders
- multiple visual motifs on one page

White space is an active design element.

---

## 2. Monumental photography

Photography should provide most of the emotional weight.

Preferred imagery:
- snow-covered alpine mountains
- medium and high peaks
- exposed geological formations
- sharp ridgelines
- layered mountain ranges
- pale or overcast skies
- distant atmospheric haze
- subdued blue-grey coloration
- large physical scale

Photography should feel documentary rather than cinematic.

### Prefer

- cold daylight
- neutral exposure
- low saturation
- hard geological texture
- wide compositions
- empty landscapes
- structural mountain forms
- asymmetrical but calm compositions

### Avoid

- sunsets
- orange skies
- dramatic HDR
- oversaturated blue
- auroras
- hikers
- climbers
- flags on summits
- cabins
- ski resorts
- dominant forests
- motivational compositions
- obvious stock photography
- fantasy mountains
- excessive fog
- artificial lens flare

The mountain is not a metaphor that needs to be explained.

It is an environment.

---

# Mountain Selection Rule

Do not choose a mountain merely because it is beautiful.

Choose it because its **geometry supports the composition**.

Look for:
- distinct ridgelines
- useful negative sky
- strong horizontal ranges
- peaks that can frame text
- geological forms that echo angular brand geometry
- sufficient visual calm for typography

Avoid iconic postcard compositions when possible.

The image should eventually become recognizable as part of a consistent
**Kurtosis mountain world**, not generic "achievement" imagery.

---

# 3. Severe geometry

Graphic forms should be:

- angular
- structural
- economical
- exact
- flat
- mathematically clean

Prefer:
- rectangles
- circles
- hard diagonals
- thin rules
- grids
- simple geometric masks
- precise charts

Avoid:
- blobs
- waves
- soft abstract shapes
- bubbly rounded UI
- excessive pill containers
- decorative curves
- playful illustration systems

Rounded elements may be used when function requires them, but they should not
become the dominant visual language.

---

# 4. Quiet typography

Typography should not compete with the subject.

Use:
- neutral grotesk or neo-grotesk sans serif
- clear hierarchy
- medium or semibold headings
- regular body copy
- compact labels
- restrained size differences
- generous line spacing where needed

Avoid:
- decorative serif fonts
- futuristic crypto fonts
- ultra-wide sci-fi typography
- excessive uppercase
- dramatic display typography
- gradients inside type
- outlining text
- unnecessary italics

Typography should feel like information being presented confidently rather than
advertising trying to attract attention.

---

# Typography Hierarchy

Default hierarchy:

1. One strong headline
2. One restrained supporting statement
3. Structured evidence or data
4. Small metadata where required

Do not create five competing text levels.

If everything looks important, the design has failed.

---

# 5. Institutional use of color

Color should communicate structure and trust rather than excitement.

Preferred family:

- deep navy
- near-black blue
- slate
- cool grey
- ice grey
- white
- restrained teal
- restrained institutional blue

Accent colors should be sparse.

A page should normally have:
- one dominant neutral
- one secondary neutral
- no more than one meaningful accent

Do not use multiple accents merely to make the design interesting.

Photography can provide visual richness instead.

---

# 6. Cold, not sterile

The aesthetic should feel cold in temperament but not lifeless.

Coldness comes from:
- snow
- navy
- slate
- white
- precise geometry
- controlled typography

Human interest comes from:
- photographic texture
- geological irregularity
- depth
- atmosphere
- real physical environments

Do not compensate for coldness with bright colors or playful illustrations.

---

# Composition

Use strong spatial hierarchy.

Preferred composition:
- one dominant image or statement
- large uninterrupted areas
- clear edges
- deliberate alignment
- few objects
- visual asymmetry within a rigorous grid

The page should be understandable from several meters away.

Ask:

**What is the first thing I see?**

There should be one clear answer.

---

# Density

Default toward lower density than conventional startup design.

However, institutional does not mean empty.

Information-heavy pages can still fit the aesthetic when they use:
- precise grids
- strong grouping
- clean tables
- small typography
- clear data hierarchy
- generous outer margins

The rule is:

**High information density is acceptable. High visual noise is not.**

---

# Photography + Text

When placing text over mountains:

- preserve legibility first
- prefer natural negative space
- use sky or quiet snow fields
- avoid covering the strongest geological feature
- avoid large artificial gradient overlays unless absolutely necessary
- crop the photograph before adding compensating visual effects

The mountain should remain visually credible as photography.

---

# Brand Geometry

When possible, create subtle relationships between:

**mountain peaks → angular brand mark → layout geometry**

These relationships should normally be perceived rather than explained.

Do not literally repeat mountain triangles throughout the design.

That becomes thematic decoration rather than taste.

---

# Slides

For presentation slides:

## Cover slides

Prefer:
- one monumental mountain image
- short title
- small metadata
- logo
- substantial negative space

Avoid:
- subtitle stacks
- multiple logos
- feature lists
- decorative charts
- unnecessary framing devices

## Content slides

Prefer:
- white or very pale neutral background
- navy typography
- restrained use of rules
- one strong chart or information structure
- minimal decoration

Use mountain photography selectively.

Do not put a mountain photograph on every slide.

The mountain is more powerful when it appears at major narrative transitions.

## Section dividers

Mountain imagery is particularly suitable for:
- opening
- chapter transition
- closing
- major thesis
- moment of strategic importance

---

# Charts and Data Visualization

Charts should look like analytical evidence rather than marketing graphics.

Prefer:
- direct labels
- thin axes
- minimal gridlines
- restrained accent color
- clear comparison
- generous margins

Avoid:
- gradients
- excessive fills
- 3D charts
- glowing lines
- decorative icons inside charts
- rainbow category palettes
- unnecessary legends

Emphasize only the data required to make the point.

Everything else should recede.

---

# Website

The website should feel closer to an institutional research publication than
a crypto landing page.

Preferred:
- strong editorial hierarchy
- substantial white space
- large but restrained photography
- concise navigation
- evidence close to claims
- clear product structure
- deep information available without overwhelming the first screen

Avoid:
- token animations
- floating coins
- glowing grids
- continuously moving backgrounds
- animated gradients
- excessive scroll effects
- "future of finance" visual clichés

Motion, when used, should be slow and functional.

---

# Social Media

Social graphics should remain recognizable even without a logo.

Achieve this through:
- consistent mountain treatment
- consistent typography
- consistent spacing
- restrained navy/white palette
- severe geometry

Do not turn social graphics into miniature advertisements.

One thought per graphic.

---

# Icons

Icons should be:
- simple
- geometric
- line-based where appropriate
- visually quiet
- consistent in weight

Avoid:
- colorful icon packs
- 3D icons
- emojis as core visual elements
- cartoon illustrations
- heavily rounded iconography

---

# Copy and Visual Relationship

The visual identity assumes the copy is equally restrained.

Prefer:
- declarative language
- specific nouns
- short claims
- evidence
- technical confidence

Avoid:
- hype
- exclamation marks
- inspirational language
- exaggerated superlatives
- excessive emoji
- slogans that explain the mountain metaphor

Never write:
"Reach new heights."
"Climb toward better yields."
"Conquer the market."

The mountain should never become a verbal gimmick.

---

# Verbal Taste

Kurtosis copy should feel like internal investment reasoning made legible, not
marketing copy dressed in institutional language.

The voice should be:

- precise
- restrained
- thesis-led
- technically fluent
- unsentimental
- calm under complexity
- confident enough not to overclaim

Prefer:

- a specific claim over a thematic label
- concrete nouns over abstractions
- active verbs over nominalizations
- one useful contrast over several benefits
- evidence close to the claim it supports
- sentence case
- buyer language when it is more exact than brand language
- short lines whose meaning remains clear without surrounding explanation

Avoid:

- vague headings such as "The insight," "Our edge," or "The opportunity" when
  the actual claim can be named
- throat-clearing such as "We believe," "At Kurtosis, we," or "In today's
  rapidly evolving landscape"
- startup vocabulary such as unlock, revolutionize, seamless, next-generation,
  game-changing, cutting-edge, supercharge, insane, or effortless
- unsupported superiority
- dramatic fragments that sound important but say little
- insider jargon used only to signal expertise
- multiple synonyms stacked for emphasis
- cleverness that makes the investment point harder to repeat internally

Good Kurtosis headlines often expose a mistaken assumption, name the position
that actually matters, or state the operating consequence. For example, replace
"The insight" with the specific thesis, such as "TVL is not underwriting."
Replace a broad claim such as "Better risk intelligence" with the actual job,
such as "Evaluate the position the fund actually owns."

Do not mechanically force every line into the "X is not Y" construction. The
strength comes from a precise distinction, not from repeating a slogan pattern.

For a short phrase:

1. Determine its intended job: thesis, transition, label, proof point, or call
   to action.
2. Recover the specific idea behind vague wording.
3. State that idea in the fewest words that preserve meaning.
4. Read it aloud. Remove anything that sounds theatrical, promotional, or
   self-conscious.
5. Check whether a sophisticated allocator could repeat it in an internal
   meeting without embarrassment.

For longer copy, develop one idea fully and preserve explicit logic. Use longer
sentences when they clarify causality, boundaries, or contrast; restraint does
not require choppy writing.

Calls to action should describe the next action plainly. Prefer "Explore
strategies," "View the methodology," or "Review the position" over manufactured
urgency.

The verbal identity should remain recognizable without mountain references,
the logo, or the phrase "institutional-grade."

---

# Premium Test

Premium does NOT mean:
- more black
- gold
- serif fonts
- thinner typography
- dramatic animation
- luxury photography

For this aesthetic, premium means:

**better editing.**

A premium version usually has fewer elements, better imagery, more deliberate
spacing, and stronger hierarchy.

---

# Crypto-Native Test

Crypto-native does NOT require:
- neon
- gradients
- purple
- token graphics
- futuristic interfaces
- Discord aesthetics

Crypto-native character can come from:
- subject matter
- terminology
- product architecture
- on-chain evidence
- concise communication

Do not visually cosplay crypto.

---

# Institutional Test

Institutional does NOT mean:
- generic corporate stock images
- office buildings
- handshakes
- suits
- excessive navy
- consulting-style diagrams everywhere

Institutional quality comes from:
- clarity
- evidence
- control
- hierarchy
- restraint
- consistency

---

# Anti-Patterns

Reject designs that feel:

### "Crypto premium"
Black background + purple gradient + glass cards + glowing token.

### "Generic fintech"
Bright blue + rounded cards + friendly illustrations.

### "Private bank"
Black + gold + serif + architecture + heritage language.

### "Consulting deck"
Dense boxes + arrows + tiny labels + multiple highlight colors.

### "Outdoor brand"
Mountain + adventurous typography + climber + lifestyle imagery.

### "Motivational finance"
Mountain peak + sunrise + aspirational slogan.

### "AI startup"
Dark UI + glowing mesh + abstract particles.

None belong to this visual system.

---

# Decision Hierarchy

When making a design decision, prioritize in this order:

1. Clarity
2. Restraint
3. Hierarchy
4. Institutional credibility
5. Technical precision
6. Distinctiveness
7. Beauty
8. Novelty

Never sacrifice the first four merely to make something visually interesting.

---

# Reduction Pass

After completing any design, perform a reduction pass.

Ask of every element:

1. Does it communicate information?
2. Does it establish hierarchy?
3. Does it strengthen the brand?
4. Would removing it make the page weaker?

If the answer to all four is no, remove it.

Then ask:

**Can one stronger element replace two weaker ones?**

Prefer that solution.

---

# Taste Evaluation

Score every design from 1–5 on:

### Restraint
Does it avoid unnecessary visual activity?

### Hierarchy
Is the intended reading order immediately obvious?

### Precision
Do spacing, alignment and geometry feel deliberate?

### Institutional credibility
Would the design look credible in front of a sophisticated allocator?

### Distinctiveness
Does it feel recognizably Kurtosis rather than generically institutional?

### Alpine coherence
When landscape imagery is used, does it belong to the defined photographic world?

### Technical character
Does it feel systematic rather than decorative?

A design should normally score at least 4/5 in every category before approval.

---

# Final Gut Check

Before approving the work, ask:

**Would a sophisticated institutional investor think this is trying too hard?**

If yes, simplify it.

Then ask:

**Would removing the logo make this look like any generic institutional company?**

If yes, strengthen the distinctive photographic, compositional, or geometric
language without adding decoration.

The final work should feel:

**monumental, precise, quiet, specialized, disciplined, and slightly austere.**

The governing principle is:

> Monumental photography. Severe geometry. Quiet typography. Almost nothing decorative.
